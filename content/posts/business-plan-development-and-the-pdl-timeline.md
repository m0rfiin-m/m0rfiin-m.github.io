When I wrote my Mastery Journey Framework at the start of the program, I gave Business Plan Development one job. It was supposed to build investor-ready business planning skills (Morfin, 2025). I also listed limited finance depth as a weakness. I expected a class about writing a plan an outsider could question. I did not expect the plan to fail its own cash test.

The structure was a four-week build, not a survey. Each week added to the same Tempest Esports plan. Weeks 1 through 3 covered the company, the industry, the target market, competition, differentiation, operations, technology, and management. Marketing and sales budgets went into one workbook. Week 4 did not open a new chapter. It asked whether Tempest could pay a crew and cover startup costs. Steven Burhoe wanted the pitch repeated at the start of each chapter, unnumbered headings, and sentences that sounded like decisions. Discussion posts ran beside the chapters, and grammar deductions were not capped.

The content changed the goal. Investor-ready does not mean a clean description of a proposed Los Angeles FPS organization. It means the cash works. The first-year sales target is $35,875. The staffing plan still has one full-time coordinator at $25 an hour and five part-time players at $22 an hour. The coordinator meets the full-time employee rule. The roster is what the operating plan needs to deliver sponsor work. Those wages do not fit inside Year 1 sales. Cumulative cash flow in the workbook stays negative through Year 2 by about $206,500. Year 3 is the first year with a profit before taxes, and that profit depends on merchandise volume that has not been tested. I came in to learn how to write the plan. I left knowing when not to hire.

I will take that test into the final business plan next month. A player hour and a coordinator paycheck are commitments. The plan can describe them. It cannot treat a projected sponsorship as money already in the bank.
