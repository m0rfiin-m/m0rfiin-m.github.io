My original PDL framework described Entertainment Media Publishing and Distribution as a course that would strengthen my understanding of platforms, releases, and distribution strategy. I do not remember the exact goal I wrote when I began the program. At the time, I viewed each course mainly as another step toward earning my master's degree and qualifying for better career opportunities. I am still deciding what kind of role I want after graduation, but this course helped me understand another side of the entertainment industry.

Before taking the course, I had considered creating a book but mostly thought about the creative part. I focused on the idea, the writing, and what the finished book might look like. I did not fully consider the work required to identify an audience, research comparable titles, find an appropriate agent, prepare a proposal, review contracts, select a distribution method, and promote the finished product. The course showed me that a creative idea is only the starting point. A book also needs a realistic business plan if it is going to reach readers.

Finding and researching an agent was one of the most useful parts of the course. For the query letter project, I had to identify an agent whose interests matched *Built for the Feed* instead of choosing a large or recognizable agency without considering whether the project fit. That process can apply beyond book publishing. If I ever work as a talent agent in sports, gaming, or media, I will need to understand the talent, research the market, identify the right opportunities, and recognize whether a relationship makes sense for both sides. The same attention to fit also applies when reviewing contracts and determining what rights, responsibilities, and expectations are involved.

The book proposal also changed how I viewed *Built for the Feed*. It could become a real project, but I would not present myself as an established expert who has already built a successful entertainment company. I am still learning. A more honest version of the book would document the process of developing an idea, testing assumptions, and learning how entertainment brands are built. That approach would allow the project to grow with my experience instead of making claims I have not earned.

I am also learning more about how I retain and apply information. Moving quickly through the program sometimes caused me to focus more on completing the immediate assignment than retaining every concept. I understand material better when I can connect it to a real responsibility, project, or workplace situation. I may not apply every publishing concept in my personal life, but I can recognize how these lessons could become useful if a future job involves talent, contracts, media projects, content distribution, or business development.

I still do not have one specific career path selected for my degree. What I have gained is a broader understanding of the roles available in entertainment and the work behind them. This course did not make me certain that I want to become a publisher or author. It gave me a more realistic understanding of what those paths require and another set of skills I can draw from when the right professional opportunity gives me a reason to apply them.
